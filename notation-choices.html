Notation Choices

This page records why the lab’s notes write certain symbols the way they do. Each entry states our convention, the reasons for it, and the other conventions you will meet in other sources. Notes sites can link to an entry here instead of keeping their own copy.

The vector mark

Our convention is a tilde under the letter, as in \(\vx\). The shared macro \vecf (and shortcuts such as \vx and \vbeta) prints it.

We chose a mark underneath for three reasons.

  • Bold letters are hard to write by hand. When we work problems by hand, for example on a board, a bold letter is hard to copy neatly with chalk or a marker. A small tilde under a letter is quick to write, so the handwritten symbol and the printed symbol look the same.
  • The space above a letter is already used. In statistics, a hat over a symbol means “estimated value”. A vector mark above the letter would have to share that space with the hat, and the two marks would be crowded together.
  • A mark below the letter stays in place when we add a hat. The vector mark stays below and the estimate mark stays above, so each mark can be read on its own.

Example 1 (An estimated vector) Suppose a regression model has a coefficient vector \(\vbeta\) with \(p\) entries. Its estimate from data is written \(\hvb\), with the hat above the symbol and the vector mark below it. We read \(\hvb\) as “the estimated vector \(\vbeta\)”. Its \(j\)-th entry is the estimate of the \(j\)-th coefficient. That entry is a single number, so it needs no vector mark, only the hat.

Other sources use bold letters (\(\mathbf{x}\)) or arrows (\(\overrightarrow{x}\)) for the same objects. All of these notations mark a vector.
